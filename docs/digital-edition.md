## Grammar Foundations

**Editorial study guide:** These explanations are additions to the digital edition for students without prior grammar study. Read the sentence roles first, then return to individual terms as they appear in the lessons. The Malayalam examples and their meanings come from the linked textbook sections; English examples illustrate the terms, not rules to translate word for word.

<a id="sentence-roles"></a>
### Words, Phrases, and Sentence Roles

A **noun** names a person, place, thing, or idea; a **pronoun** stands in for a noun, as “she” can stand in for a person's name. A **verb** expresses an action, event, or state, including “be.” An **adjective** describes a noun (“a small house”); an **adverb** adds information about an action or description (“arrives early”). A **phrase** is a group of words functioning together, such as “the small house.”

In “Ravi gives Leela a book,” identify the roles before learning their labels:

| Words | Role | What the role means here |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ravi | Subject | The giver, about whom the sentence makes a statement |
| a book | Direct object | The thing given |
| Leela | Indirect object | The recipient |
| gives Leela a book | Predicate | What the sentence says about the subject |

A subject need not perform an action: “The book is here” describes a location. Do not identify subjects only by asking “Who does it?” In Malayalam, the person who feels, wants, or knows something can take a different form from the subject of an ordinary action sentence; see [dative constructions](lesson4.md#section-4-1).

A **complement** supplies information needed to complete a construction. In “Ravi is a student,” “a student” identifies Ravi; it is not an object receiving an action. The linking verb “is” is a **copula**. Moag also calls this identifying complement a **predicate nominative**. Compare [equative sentences](lesson1.md#section-1-3), which connect two descriptions like an equation. **Existive** means expressing existence or presence, as in “There is a book”; see [ഉണ്ട്](lesson3.md#section-3-1).

**First person** means the speaker (“I/we”); **second person**, the addressee (“you”); **third person**, someone or something else (“he/she/it/they”). These labels describe grammatical perspective, not social rank. **Singular** means one; **plural**, more than one. [Malayalam pronouns](lesson1.md#section-1-6) also express social relationships.

<a id="cases"></a>
### Cases: Noun Forms and Their Uses

**Case** concerns the form a noun or pronoun takes in a construction. A **case marker** is an ending indicating such a relationship. English sometimes changes pronouns (“he/him/his”) and often uses separate words (“to Ravi,” “in the house”); Malayalam uses noun endings for many relationships. This is a helpful comparison, not a rule that each English preposition has one matching Malayalam ending.

| Term in this book | Meaning and useful starting point | Study section |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Nominative | The basic noun form, without an added case ending. Used for many subjects and also for many inanimate direct objects. | [Object contrast, §8.1](lesson8.md#section-8-1) |
| Accusative | A form marking certain objects, notably animate direct objects. “Animate” here refers to people and other living beings. | [§8.1](lesson8.md#section-8-1) |
| Dative | Often corresponds to “to” or “for,” including recipients; also used for people experiencing certain feelings, wants, or abilities. | [§4.1](lesson4.md#section-4-1), [§4.4](lesson4.md#section-4-4) |
| Genitive / possessive | Marks possession or another relationship between nouns: “father's office,” “the meaning of the word.” | [§6.6](lesson6.md#section-6-6) |
| Locative | Expresses location, often “in,” “on,” or “at.” | [§2.1](lesson2.md#section-2-1) |
| Addressive / associative | Two labels used here for the -ോട് form: used for the person told or asked, and in certain expressions of association. | [§8.3](lesson8.md#section-8-3) |
| Instrumental | Can express the means by which something happens, “with/by means of”; also has other uses explained later. | [§20.4](lesson20.md#section-20-4) |
| Vocative | Used when calling to someone directly. It addresses the listener rather than stating something about them. | [§21.3](lesson21.md#section-21-3) |

**Form and role are different.** “Direct object” names a sentence role; “accusative” names a form. In [§8.1](lesson8.md#section-8-1), കാപ്പി (“coffee”) is the object in “I will bring coffee” but stays in the nominative, while രവിയെ (“Ravi”) has the accusative ending in “I will bring Ravi.” Likewise, dative is not a tense: noun case and verb tense answer different questions.

For an experience, [§4.1](lesson4.md#section-4-1) gives എനിക്ക് ഒരു പേന വേണം, “I want/need a pen”: എനിക്ക് identifies the person with the need, ഒരു പേന the needed item, and വേണം the want or need. The English translation uses “I”; Malayalam uses the dative form എനിക്ക്. An English subject does not automatically become a Malayalam nominative.

A **postposition** is a relationship word placed after its noun phrase, unlike an English preposition placed before it. In വീട്ടിൽ നിന്ന്, “from the house” ([§6.5](lesson6.md#section-6-5)), വീട്ടിൽ is already locative and നിന്ന് follows it. Learn the noun form together with its postposition.

### Tense, Aspect, Mood, and Verb Forms

These terms describe different features of verbs:

- **Tense** relates an event or state to time: compare “walks,” “walked,” and “will walk.” Malayalam present, past, and future forms have their own ranges of use; the -ഉം form also expresses habits and general truths ([§7.1](lesson7.md#section-7-1)). Choose by meaning and context, not just the English tense label.
- **Aspect** concerns how an event is presented: as ongoing, completed, repeated, or having a continuing result. Compare English “was reading” with “read.” Both can concern past time. See [progressive aspect](lesson21.md#section-21-6) and the [stative perfect](lesson13.md#section-13-1), which connects a completed event with its continuing effects.
- **Mood and modality** concern meanings such as commands, intentions, possibility, or necessity. **Imperative** means a command or request; **potential** concerns possibility or ability; **desiderative** concerns wanting or obligation in the constructions taught here. These labels do not name additional past/present/future tenses. See [-ാം](lesson6.md#section-6-3) and [-ണം](lesson8.md#section-8-4).

An **ending**, or **suffix**, attaches after a word or stem; a **prefix** attaches before it. A **stem** is the part to which grammatical endings attach. A **citation form** is the form used to name a word, as in a dictionary; the book commonly lists verbs in -ഉക. A dictionary form and an infinitive need not be the same form.

An **infinitive** is a verb form used within a larger construction, often to name an intended action. In ഞങ്ങൾ സിനിമ കാണാൻ പോകുന്നു, “We are going (in order) to see a movie” ([§5.2](lesson5.md#section-5-2)), കാണാൻ expresses the purpose, “to see”; പോകുന്നു is the main verb, “are going.” **Finite** forms can serve as the main verb of a clause; **nonfinite** forms depend on another construction rather than independently supplying the main assertion. English “to” and “-ing” are clues, not reliable conversion rules for Malayalam.

A **transitive** verb takes a direct object: “She opened the door.” An **intransitive** verb does not: “The door opened.” This distinction concerns the verb's construction, not whether an object happens to be spoken aloud; context can leave an object understood. A **causative** expresses causing someone or something to act or change, as “make someone walk.” See the contrasts in [§20.7](lesson20.md#section-20-7).

<a id="clauses-and-derived-forms"></a>
### Clauses, Participles, and Verbal Nouns

A **clause** expresses an event or state, usually through a verb and its associated words. A sentence may contain several clauses. A **main clause** supplies the central assertion; a **subordinate** or **embedded clause** functions within a larger construction. A **relative clause** describes a noun: in “the student who studies here,” “who studies here” identifies the student. Malayalam places the descriptive clause before its noun ([§8.2](lesson8.md#section-8-2)). The noun described is sometimes called the **head noun**.

A **participle** is a verb form used to modify a noun, connect actions, or help build a larger verb expression. Its function depends on the construction. The **conjunctive participle** links actions ([§13.2–3](lesson13.md#section-13-2)); a **verbal adjective** describes a noun ([§13.5](lesson13.md#section-13-5)). A **verbal noun** allows an action or event to occupy a noun's place, for example as the subject or object, or before a postposition ([§12.4](lesson12.md#section-12-4)). **Nominalization** means making such a noun construction. English “-ing” can have several functions, so identify the role before choosing a Malayalam form.

An **auxiliary** is a helping verb used with another verb. A **compound verb** combines verbs into one larger expression; the additional verb can contribute completion, continuation, or another meaning rather than its ordinary independent meaning ([§11.3](lesson11.md#section-11-3)). **Active** and **passive voice** organize the participants differently: compare “Ravi opened the door” and “The door was opened by Ravi.” The Malayalam construction is taught in [§20.5](lesson20.md#section-20-5).

A **conditional** presents an “if” relationship ([§14.2](lesson14.md#section-14-2)). **Negation** makes an expression negative. **Focus** emphasizes a selected part of a message; a **cleft sentence** does this by restructuring it, like English “It was Ravi who came” ([§11.2](lesson11.md#section-11-2)). These are ways of organizing meaning, not noun cases.

<a id="grammar-check"></a>
### Check Your Understanding

Before memorizing an ending, check that you can identify the meaning or role it expresses.

1. In “Leela gives Ravi a book,” who is the recipient, and what is the direct object?
2. In “The door opened,” is “the door” a direct object because it undergoes a change?
3. Must a Malayalam direct object always have an accusative ending?
4. What does the contrast between “was reading” and “will be reading” change: time, ongoing aspect, or both?
5. In the §5.2 movie example above, which form expresses purpose, and which is the main verb?

**Answers and reasons:** (1) Ravi is the recipient/indirect object; the book is the direct object. (2) No. It is the subject of an intransitive verb; undergoing change does not by itself make something an object. (3) No. The coffee example in §8.1 has a nominative direct object. (4) Time changes; both present the action as ongoing. (5) കാണാൻ expresses purpose; പോകുന്നു is the main verb. Return to the relevant section if identifying the role is harder than recognizing the ending.
